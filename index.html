Job Market Paper

The Organizational Tradeoff of AI Coordination: Evidence from a Container Port

M.D. Ma (2026)

Awards: SMS Best Conference PhD Paper Prize · AOM Robert J. Litschert Best Doctoral Student Paper Award · AOM STR Division Best Paper Award · AOM William H. Newman Dissertation Award (top-three finalist)

Abstract

Centralization can improve operational efficiency by coordinating interdependent activities, yet impede adaptation when changing local conditions must be communicated and interpreted at the center. A centralized AI system that aggregates local information, processes it in real time, and relays revised instructions to each unit may ease these demands. I study one of the world's largest container terminals, where the same prescriptive AI system dispatches autonomous trucks through zones used almost exclusively by them (pure-AI) or shared with independent human drivers (human–AI). Across 179,925 tasks, pure-AI zones move 11.2 percent more cargo per vehicle in ordinary operations. After recurrent mechanical faults, they recover more slowly, equalizing per-vehicle productivity. During unprecedented geomagnetic storms, they lift 13 percent less cargo per crane-hour than human–AI zones. Exploratory telemetry indicates that human drivers responded to the storms in varied ways and autonomous trucks adjusted around them, yielding a better collective response than AI coordination alone. The findings suggest an organizational trade-off: AI control raises efficiency in ordinary operations, while human discretion helps the system adapt to unprecedented disruptions.

Generative AI and the Architecture of Knowledge Work: Expertise Bypass, Exchange Governability, and Shifting Bottlenecks

M.G. Jacobides, M.D. Ma, A. Das, S. Iyer, M. Langione (2026). Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal.

Abstract

Research on GenAI and work has concentrated at the task level. We ask when task-level effects alter knowledge-work industry architecture: the allocation of activities across firm boundaries. Using a three-stage abductive design - an executive roundtable, a panel of 626 firm websites (2017–2025), and 33 interviews plus two further roundtables with C-level executives and corporate directors - we document divergent repositioning: professional services firms foreground relational value, while interface-oriented technology firms emphasize scalable interfaces. We find that activity reallocation is shaped by two independently varying dimensions: expertise bypass and exchange governability. Their intersection yields four configurations: contestable reallocation, persistent bundles with selective insourcing, federated expert provision, and integrated expert provision. We extend industry architecture theory by explaining change when production and exchange conditions shift separately.

Working Papers

What Are Generative AI's Competitive Implications? A Longitudinal Documentation of Corporate Director Expectations

M.G. Jacobides, M.D. Ma (2026). 2nd Round Reject & Resubmit at Strategic Management Journal.

Abstract

How do senior decision-makers assess the competitive implications of GenAI? Using an abductive, multi-stage study of 531 UK corporate directors (exploratory roundtables, survey, matched stimulus roundtables, and follow-up interviews, 2024–2026), we document that directors distinguish three dimensions of GenAI's competitive implications: competitive differentiation, firm displacement, and industry disruption. Expectations vary markedly even within the same sub-sector. This heterogeneity is patterned by how directors frame their firms' value propositions - especially perceived modularity and reliance on pattern recognition - and by complements and buffers such as proprietary data, relationship-intensive delivery, and regulatory/liability constraints. Across waves, directors' competitive expectations remain stable, consistent with durable interpretive frames during early diffusion.

The Value of 'Human-in-the-Loop': Evidence from Self-Driving Logistics

M.D. Ma, F. Brahm, S. Sun (2026)

Abstract

AI systems increasingly perform tasks once done by humans. Yet humans are often kept "in the loop" to improve performance through selective intervention, even when tasks can be completed autonomously. We study the value of human intervention at one of the world's largest ports, located in China, where remote operators can take over an autonomous truck stuck for two minutes. We exploit a capacity constraint as a quasi-natural experiment: because only one truck can be controlled at a time, trucks that become stuck while the operator is controlling another truck are left to resolve the exception autonomously (control group), and we compare them with trucks that receive takeover (treatment group); we match the groups on pre-treatment operational characteristics. Human takeover reduces task completion time by roughly 8% relative to AI-alone resolution, with the largest benefits at busy cranes with few other stuck trucks. Operators' choices, however, do not consistently track these benefits, a pattern consistent with limited feedback: because the system can eventually resolve exceptions on its own, a completed task does not reveal whether intervention helped. Our results extend the management-by-exception logic of Garicano (2000): in autonomous systems, which exceptions merit human intervention is not obvious, and operators may lack a basis to learn it from experience.

Realizing Physical and Digital Synergies in Multi-Business Firms: A Configurational Approach

M.G. Jacobides, M.D. Ma (2026)

Abstract

We examine how digital infrastructure transforms coordination mechanisms and synergy patterns within multi-business firms. Through a longitudinal study of a large retail conglomerate's evolution from traditional M-form to internal ecosystem, we document a three-stage process whereby digital capabilities progressively alter value creation dynamics. Our findings reveal how digital infrastructure enables systematic lateral coordination between previously unrelated businesses, replacing sporadic collaboration with data-driven discovery of synergistic opportunities. We identify novel forms of value creation - superadditive demand synergies and data-network effects - that differ from traditional resource-based relatedness. The corporate center's role evolves from orchestrating synergies to maintaining digital infrastructure while enabling autonomous collaboration. These findings explain how digitally enabled firms successfully pursue broader, seemingly unrelated scope through fundamentally different governance mechanisms than traditional conglomerates.

Generative AI and the Shifting Locus of Innovation

M.D. Ma, K. Vakili. Data analysis in progress; IEPC funded.

Overview

GenAI lowers the cost of producing software, but its uneven productivity gains may also change the direction of innovation. We examine whether these differences redirect effort away from upstream exploration toward downstream exploitation. Using data from one of the largest open-source repositories, we trace programming-language births, usage, and project creation. Comparing venture-backed start-ups with established incumbents, and leveraging variation in GenAI performance across languages, we investigate who reallocates effort, who reskills, and how innovation patterns evolve.

Generative AI and Schumpeterian Competition

M.D. Ma, S. Puntoni, M.G. Jacobides. Draft in preparation; Wharton AI & Analytics funded.

Overview

GenAI may reshape the balance between innovation led by new entrants and innovation built on incumbents' accumulated capabilities. This conceptual paper examines how changes in the production and application of knowledge may alter the logic of competition within established technological regimes. We develop a theoretical account of how these changes could reshape incentives to innovate and the conditions under which technological change creates opportunities for challengers or reinforces incumbent advantage.

Task Interdependence and the Attribution of AI's Value

K. McElheran, M.D. Ma. In design; experimental platform built.

Overview

When organizational output depends on interdependent tasks, improvements in performance can be difficult to attribute to AI, human effort, or their interaction. Using a purpose-built team-production platform, we experimentally examine how task interdependence shapes managers' ability to assess AI's contribution to joint output. The project investigates when interdependence creates causal ambiguity and what this means for organizations' ability to recognize and evaluate the value of AI.

AI Agents and Entrepreneurial Performance

R. Clarke, M.D. Ma. In design; implementation partner secured.

Overview

AI agents can carry out activities within a business, extending the potential of AI from providing advice to executing tasks. In partnership with a major South African payments platform, we are designing a randomized field experiment to evaluate the effects of embedded AI agents on small-firm performance. The study will examine whether these agents improve business outcomes, whether gains are broadly distributed or concentrated among particular firms, and the channels through which those gains arise.

Generative AI and Knowledge Hierarchies Inside Firms

M.D. Ma, R. Clarke, E.Y. Cheng. In design; data partnership secured.

Overview

GenAI may reshape organizational hierarchies by changing where knowledge is accessed and problems are resolved. Using enterprise telemetry, we examine how GenAI changes the flow of questions and exceptions within firms: which are resolved locally, which are escalated upward, and which are delegated downward. The project investigates how these shifts redistribute problem-solving responsibilities and what they imply for the organization of expertise and the structure of firms.
